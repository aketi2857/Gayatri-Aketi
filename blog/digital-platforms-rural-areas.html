How Successful Are Digital Platforms in Rural Areas

Lessons from my 2023 survey of Telangana Sona farmers

By Aketi Gayatri Jahnavi, October 2026. About 9 minutes to read.

In 2023, I conducted a survey of Telangana Sona farmers in Nidamanuru, Kanagal and Adavidevulapalli mandals. The survey examined phone use, awareness of electronic agricultural markets and respondents' reasons for choosing physical markets. It revealed a gap between access to a phone and awareness of platforms that could support the sale of agricultural produce.

Mobile phone ownership stood at 98.4%, while e-platform awareness reached 25.9% among completed awareness responses. Among phone owners who answered the awareness question, 73.7% were unaware. The presence of a device therefore offered limited evidence about a farmer's readiness to explore a digital agricultural market.

These findings raise a practical question about success. A platform may exist and a farmer may own a phone, yet a sale still depends on understanding the offer, trusting the terms and receiving the agreed payment. My survey identified barriers at this point, although it could not establish whether digital platforms improved income or enabled successful transactions.

The gap between access and practical awareness

Among completed awareness responses, 74.1% were unaware of e-platforms. In answers explaining physical market use, 70.5% gave 'Lack of Awareness' as the reason. These percentages concern different questions, but both indicate that knowledge of digital agricultural markets was limited.

A useful awareness programme should explain the sale process. Farmers need to understand how their produce enters a market, how quality affects a bid, which charges apply and how payment is confirmed. Knowing a platform's name offers little practical help if the farmer cannot work out what an offer means for the final amount received.

The survey also showed why phone ownership should be interpreted carefully. It did not establish connection quality, reliable internet access or the ability to trade independently. Those questions require separate measurement. They should not be assumed to have been resolved simply because a respondent reported owning a mobile phone.

Education patterns add another dimension. Among respondents recorded as illiterate, phone ownership stood at 93.3%, while recorded e-platform awareness was 0.0%. Awareness reached 9.1% among completed responses in the primary education category. These comparisons describe unequal groups and do not establish that education caused awareness. They do suggest a need to test forms of assistance that are accessible to people with different levels of comfort with written instructions.

Spoken explanations in Telugu and demonstrations of a real sale could make the process easier to follow. Farmers should be able to ask questions about an offer and check the answer against an actual sale record. Whether such support improves participation would need to be evaluated through completed transactions.

Trust and the appeal of familiar markets

Awareness did not remove the appeal of established trading relationships. Among respondents who knew about e-platforms, 60.0% gave either trust and a cultural relationship with a middleman, or shared caste with a middleman, as their recorded reason for using physical markets. Within this aware subgroup, 26.7% gave the former reason and 33.3% the latter.

These responses suggest that a farmer may understand that another channel exists and still prefer a familiar person. A known trading relationship can feel easier to evaluate than an unfamiliar process. The survey records that preference, but it does not prove that relationships caused farmers to avoid digital trade.

The practical task is to make alternative transactions clear and dependable. Farmers need to know who is responsible for payment, what happens if the agreed terms change and how they can raise a problem. A platform should make these arrangements understandable regardless of the farmer's social connections. Shared caste, as reported in the survey, is a basis for choosing a relationship rather than evidence that the transaction is fair.

Other responses mentioned uncertainty about full payment, commodity security, storage and costs associated with bidding. These concerns were not coded into consistent categories, so reliable percentages cannot be assigned to each of them. They nevertheless identify practical issues that an awareness campaign alone may leave unresolved.

An attractive price is only part of an offer. The farmer also needs to understand deductions, delivery responsibilities and the promised payment date. The survey includes a response describing perceived profits from physical and electronic channels as similar. This is a reported perception, rather than a verified profitability comparison, but it helps explain why a farmer might see limited reason to change a familiar arrangement.

A different starting point in each village

The overall awareness percentage concealed sharp differences between villages. Dorepalli recorded 80.0% awareness, while Shabdullapuram and Ulshayapalem recorded 0.0% among their surveyed responses. Phone ownership in the latter village samples stood at 100.0%. Access to a device therefore coexisted with very different levels of market awareness.

The survey does not establish what produced these differences. Exposure to demonstrations, access to assistance or experience of a successful sale could be relevant. A useful follow-up would investigate what respondents in Dorepalli had encountered and what information was missing elsewhere.

This matters for programme design. A common message may reach places with different starting conditions. Local assistance should begin by understanding what farmers already know, what they find unclear and whether help is available when they attempt a transaction.

Using familiar digital habits to support learning

The survey also asked which app respondents mostly used. Among completed responses, YouTube accounted for 43.3%, Facebook for 23.3%, WhatsApp for 21.7% and Moj for 11.7%. These preferences identify possible channels for practical market information.

The percentages do not show whether farmers used these apps for agricultural information or how much time they spent on them. They do show that familiar digital channels were part of reported phone use. Short demonstrations in Telugu could introduce offer comparison, quality assessment and confirmation of payment through those channels.

A demonstration should be linked to support at the point of sale. A farmer who understands a video may still need help checking deductions or responding to a disputed quality assessment. An evaluation should therefore measure whether information leads to a completed transaction, rather than treating viewing or registration as sufficient evidence of success.

Connecting digital information with the actual sale

Physical and digital markets can operate together. eNAM's official description explains that its electronic portal connects existing agricultural produce markets, with transparent price discovery and timely online payment as stated objectives.[2] A farmer may visit a mandi while parts of the sale are handled electronically.

Preference for a physical market therefore does not necessarily establish rejection of digital processes. A follow-up should examine the actual sale: how the bid was obtained, who explained it, who accepted it and how payment arrived. The present survey did not consistently record those steps.

A practical improvement suggested by the findings is an assisted comparison of complete offers. Before accepting a sale, the farmer would receive a short record showing the agreed quantity and quality, quoted price, itemised charges, expected amount received and promised payment date. The contact for handling a problem would be clearly identified.

A paper copy and a phone message could present the same terms. Spoken explanation would be available when needed, and changes to deductions or quality assessment would be explained before acceptance. The farmer would retain control over the decision.

The comparison should include selling and delivery costs. A higher quoted bid can leave a lower amount after charges. Offers should also concern equivalent quantity and quality. The survey does not establish which channel provided the best complete terms, so the value of this proposal would need to be tested.

A sale record would need dependable procedures behind it. Payment delays, disputed weights and changed deductions should have a clear route for resolution. Local assistance could help a farmer review the terms and check whether the payment matched the agreement.

Farmer producer organisations could explore whether grouping produce or arranging transport makes participation more useful. eNAM's official guidance describes organisations aggregating produce for electronic trading, receiving payment and distributing it to members.[3] Any local arrangement would need clear records of members' produce, charges and shares of payment. Its effect on returns in the surveyed villages remains a question for evaluation.

Judging success through outcomes

My survey measured awareness and stated market preferences. It did not provide a consistent record of completed electronic sales, payment timeliness or repeat participation. Net-return entries were unrecorded in 98.4% of the survey records. The available evidence therefore cannot establish whether digital platforms improved profitability.

A stronger evaluation would report the percentage of eligible participants who completed a trade, the percentage paid by the agreed date and the percentage who voluntarily used the service again. It would compare amounts received after selling charges for equivalent produce. Farmers' ability to understand and accept the terms should also be checked.

Such a study could compare ordinary market information with additional assistance through a real sale. The assisted group would receive help reviewing complete offers and following payment. With an appropriate design, the study could test whether this support improved participation and sale outcomes.

The findings relate to surveyed respondents in 2023. The sampling procedure is unspecified, subgroup sizes are unequal and some responses are missing. Internet quality, affordability and access to banking were not consistently measured. These are useful questions for further enquiry, but should not be presented as established causes of the observed awareness gap.

Continued preference for a physical market should remain a valid outcome when it offers better terms or dependable service. The purpose of digital market support should be to improve the farmer's available choices. A change of channel by itself does not establish a better result.

Conclusion

My 2023 survey shows a clear gap between mobile phone ownership and awareness of digital agricultural markets. Phone ownership stood at 98.4%, while e-platform awareness reached 25.9% among completed awareness responses. Farmers' answers pointed to limited awareness, reliance on familiar trading relationships, and concerns about payment and the security of produce.

Improving participation requires practical guidance and dependable transactions. Demonstrations in Telugu and local assistance could help farmers understand offers, compare selling costs and check payment. Clear deductions, agreed payment dates and accessible support would make a sale easier to evaluate before acceptance.

The survey does not establish whether digital platforms improved income or enabled successful sales. Their success should therefore be assessed through completed transactions, timely payments, returns after selling costs and voluntary repeat use. The goal should be to give farmers better market choices and confidence in the terms of each sale.

Data note

Survey findings are reported as percentages of completed answers for the relevant question or subgroup unless stated otherwise. Unrecorded answers remain missing. Awareness coding is interpreted as aware or unaware and requires confirmation from the questionnaire before formal publication. The survey year of 2023 was supplied by the author. The 'Lack of Awareness' percentage uses the exact recorded wording after trimming spaces and ignoring case. Official platform descriptions provide context and do not establish which functions respondents used in 2023.
